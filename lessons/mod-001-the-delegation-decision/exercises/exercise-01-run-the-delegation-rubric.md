# Exercise 01: Run the Delegation Rubric on This Week's Work

**Module:** [Module 01: The Delegation Decision](../README.md) · **Time:** 45–60 minutes · **Output:** a filled rubric for 10–15 tasks and a one-page reflection

## Goal

Move the four-variable rubric from the page into a habit by running it against real tasks from your own queue. You are not trying to be right on the first pass. You are trying to discover where your calibration is off, so you can tighten it next week.

## Steps

1. **List tasks.** Open your calendar, your to-do list, and the last five days of messages and meetings. Write down every PM task you did or are about to do that took (or will take) more than fifteen minutes. Aim for 10–15 tasks. Include the boring ones; especially include the boring ones.
2. **Score each task.** For each task, score the four variables — verifiability, context availability, reversibility, judgment load — from 1 to 3. Compute `V + C + R - J`. Use the rubric in the module README to pick a move.
3. **Compare to what you actually did.** For each past task, note whether you delegated it to a model, partly delegated, or kept it entirely. Where does the rubric's recommendation disagree with what you did?
4. **Find one over-delegation and one under-delegation.** Pick the clearest example of each from your week. Write two sentences on each: what went wrong (or would have gone wrong if nobody had caught it), and what the rubric would have had you do instead.
5. **Pick three upcoming tasks.** Choose three tasks on your plate for the coming week, score them now, and commit to the move the rubric recommends. Put a note in your calendar one week out to check how each went.
6. **Write the one-pager.** In one page, answer three questions: (a) Where does my gut mostly agree with the rubric? (b) Where does it reliably disagree, and is my gut or the rubric closer to right? (c) Which of the four variables do I consistently misjudge?

## What good looks like

- Your list includes at least one task you would normally not think of as "AI-relevant" (a 1:1 prep, an estimate, a meeting you were about to run). The rubric still has an answer.
- The two cases in step 4 are specific enough to tell a colleague about in under a minute, with the actual output or decision attached.
- The one-pager names at least one calibration error with evidence, not a general feeling. _"I under-delegate transcripts because I keep thinking I'll read them live"_ beats _"I under-delegate sometimes."_
- At least one of your three upcoming tasks produces a `≤ 1` score. If every task you picked scored 5+, your sample is biased toward easy wins and you will not learn anything useful from the week-later check.

## Keep it

Save the filled rubric and the one-pager. In one week, re-score the three upcoming tasks you committed to and compare: did the rubric's recommendation hold up? Where it didn't, which variable was misjudged? The goal of the exercise is not to lock in a rubric; it is to give you a loop for tuning your own judgment.

You will reuse this habit in later modules. The next module builds on it by turning "delegate the draft" into a working practice for briefing the model and writing PRDs that double as agent context. You will need the triage fluent before that practice is worth the setup cost.
